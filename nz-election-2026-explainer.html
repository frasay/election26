Step 1

Parliament normally has 120 seats.

Every voter gets two votes: one for a local MP, one for a party. There are 71 local (electorate) seats, and the rest are filled from party lists so the final result matches the party vote.

With 120 seats, a government needs 61.

Step 2

This time it has 131.

When a party wins more local seats than its party vote would give it, it keeps them all, and Parliament grows to make room. That is an overhang.

National won 42 local seats but its 29.2% party vote earns about 35. Te Pāti Māori won five but earns one. That is 7 plus 4, so 11 extra seats, the biggest overhang since MMP began in 1996.

Step 3

So the finish line moved to 66.

More seats means a higher majority. On the preliminary count, National, ACT and NZ First have 67. Labour, the Greens and Te Pāti Māori have 57. The Opportunity Party has 7.

The right bloc is one seat over the line. Not much of a cushion.

Step 4

Then come the special votes.

Election night leaves out special votes: ballots cast overseas, outside the voter's own electorate, or by people on the unpublished roll.

About 399,365 are still to count. That is roughly one in seven of all votes cast.

Step 5

Some seats can move. Some can't.

National has no list seats at all. Every one of its 42 seats is a local win, so a lower party vote does not cost it seats.

The flickering dots are list seats. These are the ones special votes can shift, and they belong to Labour, the Greens, ACT, NZ First and Opportunity. The faded dots only change if a local result flips.

Step 6

The old pattern may not hold.

In 2017, 2020 and 2023, special votes took two seats off National each time. Usually that helps the left.

But enrolment now closes three weeks before election day, which cuts out the late enrollers who drove those swings. RNZ estimates the left and Opportunity would need to beat their election night result by about seven points to win a majority.

How the model works
  • Seats follow New Zealand's method (Sainte-Laguë): 120 seats shared by party vote among parties with 5% or a local seat.
  • A party keeps every local seat it wins. Any excess over its share is the overhang, and it is added on top of the 120.
  • The 399,365 special votes are treated as party votes. Each party's share is its election night share multiplied by its 2023 special-vote ratio (Greens 1.36, Te Pāti Māori 1.88, Opportunity 1.34, Labour 1.01, National 0.89, ACT 0.81, NZ First 0.72), scaled by the first slider.
  • Local results stay as they were on election night, apart from any flips you choose. Special votes also affect local results in real life, so treat this as an illustration.
  • Left bloc is Labour, Greens and Te Pāti Māori. Right bloc is National, ACT and NZ First. Real coalitions are only fixed once a government forms.
04 / What happens next

The road to27 November.

--days until the official result is expected.
Sat 7 Nov

Election day

Preliminary results count 2,432,403 ordinary votes. Neither Christopher Luxon nor Chris Hipkins claims victory.

From Sun 8 Nov

The official count begins

Rolls are scanned for double voting, all election day votes are recounted, and special votes cast in New Zealand are sent back to their home electorate. Luxon's government stays in caretaker mode.

Ten days

Overseas votes arrive

The law allows ten days for votes cast overseas to come back to be counted.

Fri 27 Nov

Official results Big day

The Electoral Commission is expected to declare the final result. This is when the overhang, the majority line and the seats for every party are locked in.

Within three days

Recount window

A recount can be requested within three days of the official result.

Thu 3 Dec

Return of the writ

The formal paperwork confirming the election result is due back.

After the final count

Coalition talks

Negotiations are likely to follow the final count. If neither bloc has a majority on its own, the Opportunity Party's seven seats could matter a lot.

By Thu 14 Jan 2027

Parliament must meet

Add coalition talks, and the country could be well into summer before a government is confirmed.
